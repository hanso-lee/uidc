Here, welfare refers to the minimum guarantee of economic outcomes discussed in Chapter 9.2. Then, how could we integrate welfare into currency? Imagine we had magic; we could wave a wand crafted from a Hungarian dragon’s tail spike over a wallet, infusing it with magic. Every night at the stroke of midnight, the enchanted wallet would conjure magical money out of thin air. We could create many such wallets and distribute them to everyone. While we’re at it, we might as well give them to people all around the world. We’ll call this money UIDC, and its unit IDC, until we come up with a better name. In ancient times, to create UIDC, witches would have been tasked with producing enchanted wallets instead of being hunted and burned. With dragons and witches extinct, we now wield information technology that works almost like magic at our fingertips.


UIDC (Universal Issuance Demurrage Currency, informally Universal Issuance Decay Currency) is a digital currency implemented primarily through computer networks. Fortunately, people have already become accustomed to seeing numbers on screens as money (Chapter 8.2). Even for myself, using a credit card is much more common than handling cash in daily life. I often check my account balance on my smartphone to see if a credit card payment has gone through, feeling as if I have an invisible wallet. With the relatively long history of internet banking and the rise of cryptocurrencies since 2009, the concept of an electronic wallet no longer feels foreign.


UIDC requires two types of electronic wallets. One is a regular wallet, while the other is a citizen wallet, of which each person can have only one. At midnight every day, 1 IDC is issued (created) in each citizen wallet. (If all wallets generated UIDC, people would create countless wallets to get more money.) To avoid geographical biases in system operation, we could use a neutral time standard, like that of the International Space Station.


If UIDC is issued daily, the amount of money will increase indefinitely, resulting in inflation (a rise in prices) that reduces the currency’s purchasing power. The value of the daily 1 IDC issued would also decline over time. To counteract this, a fixed proportion of money is removed from all wallets each day. (If some wallets were exempt, people would concentrate their money in those wallets. If a fixed amount disappeared from each wallet instead of a proportion, people would also move their money around; with a progressive rate, they’d disperse it across as many wallets as possible.) Of course, this proportion must be chosen with great care. Here, just to keep the math simple and easy to remember, let’s say that 0.1% (1/1000) of the balance in every wallet disappears daily. Here’s what happens at midnight (UTC+0) every day:


Regular wallet: 0.1% of the UIDC balance disappears.

Citizen wallet: 0.1% of the UIDC balance disappears, and 1 IDC is issued.


If someone holds 0 IDC before midnight, their balance becomes 1 IDC (0 × 0.999 + 1). Someone with 100 IDC will have 100.9 IDC (100 × 0.999 + 1). 1,000 IDC remains 1,000 IDC (1,000 × 0.999 + 1), while 10,000 IDC reduces to 9,991 IDC (10,000 × 0.999 + 1). (See chart in The Selected Ethic, p. 275)


Those holding less than 1,000 IDC will see their balance increase, while those with more than 1,000 IDC will see it decrease. Looking at the final figures, this appears to result in something similar to treating currency holdings as ability to pay (Chapter 9.3), taxing accordingly, and funding welfare programs. Over time, the total balance across all wallets will converge to the number of citizen wallets multiplied by 1,000 IDC. (See proof in The Selected Ethic, p. 276)


Readers may recognize similarities to the negative income tax proposed by Milton Friedman or the basic income plan from 2020 U.S. Democratic presidential candidate Andrew Yang. Many ideas similar to UIDC exist, differing in specific implementation details. Even the concept of decreasing money was proposed around 100 years ago by businessman Silvio Gesell, who called it "Freigeld."


Applying this system to today’s cash-based currency would be highly cumbersome. Imagine hiring a vast number of civil servants to track everyone’s cash holdings daily and imposing taxes. Administrative costs (Chapter 9.1) would soar, and people would evade taxes by hiding cash from inspectors.


In contrast, UIDC has no corresponding physical form, and the disappearance of currency happens automatically at the designated time, making it unavoidable. Recall the content in Chapter 8.5. The money someone earns from selling goods is retained as their property, which is used for reinvestment in capital or to purchase other goods. The disappearing nature of UIDC, combined with people's profit motives (Chapter 8.2), will minimize the time money is held, stimulating investment and consumption. Not using money leads to loss, as retained funds diminish. In the case of UIDC, we might observe a new phase of money that, like superfluid helium flowing up the walls and spilling out of its container, escapes the liquidity trap even in deflation.


In medieval France, feudal lords (Seigneurs) issued currency. From this practice comes the term "seigniorage," which refers to the profit a government earns by issuing currency. For example, if the cost of printing a one-dollar bill (100 cents) is 10 cents, the remaining 90 cents (100-10) is the seigniorage the government retains. UIDC effectively returns the right to issue currency to citizens, enabling them to mint currency equally and claim seigniorage. With issuance and decay managed automatically through technology, opportunities for government corruption (Chapter 7.3) and administrative costs are minimized.


I’d be pleased if UIDC merely served as a spark for other innovative ideas. In fact, if UIDC were realized precisely as described here, I’d be more worried than enthusiastic. Preventing potential chaos requires thorough research, examination, and preparation of supplementary measures, with particular attention to setting an appropriate decay rate and addressing issues like privacy, power outages, and other emergencies. Let’s discuss a few considerations here.


A first criticism might be that the world isn’t unified enough to share a single currency. According to the Optimal Currency Area theory, the advantages of using a common currency increase with economic and political integration and freedom of exchange. Thus, the benefits of UIDC should grow as global interactions and integration increase.


Frankly speaking, suggesting the use of UIDC worldwide is largely an exercise in imagination. In practical terms, the most plausible scenario is its introduction as a parallel currency within a country already politically unified. Even if it were to be implemented internationally, it would only be feasible among countries that are already well-integrated—namely, the major advanced countries with established structures that facilitate cooperation and where active exchange and collaboration are already taking place (Chapter 9.4). The value of money ultimately comes from the productivity of those who receive it (Chapter 8.2). For UIDC to have a greater welfare effect, in other words, for the UIDC issued daily from citizen wallets to hold more value, it needs to be adopted as legal tender (Chapter 8.4) in major advanced countries with high productivity built on accumulated capital (Chapter 8.1).


Once UIDC is designated as legal tender in major advanced countries, people in other countries must also be able to obtain citizen wallets for it to expand globally. (If this expansion does not occur, demand for migration to advanced countries to receive welfare benefits would surge. If it does, such demand would likely decline from current levels.) This requires establishing an international organization (or organizations) that can operate across borders to guarantee people’s identities or oversee the identity-guarantee process. Issuing citizen wallets is not too difficult in advanced countries with sufficient administrative power, but it poses problems in countries where the government is not trustworthy. Advanced countries already have well-established means of identity verification, such as driver's licenses and passports, that can be easily adapted for issuing citizen wallets. However, if the issuance of citizen wallets is left to an untrustworthy government, some people may not be able to get their wallets due to unproven identities, or corrupt governments may forge false identities to exploit the wallets. To prevent such situations, reliable international organizations are necessary. However, even in this case, it's difficult for closed countries to participate in UIDC.


There are also various technical considerations. UIDC can be implemented using existing client-server structures or by adopting distributed ledger technology that is popular these days. Additionally, taking advantage of digital currency allows for setting minimal units down to the decimal. For instance, we could use 1 mIDC (0.001 IDC) as the smallest unit, and if the currency's purchasing power increases, we could switch to 1 μIDC (0.000001 IDC). To prevent system overload, a small transaction fee could be set for each transfer, or it could be removed if processing capacity becomes sufficient.


A daily (24-hour) issuance and destruction cycle seems appropriate. If this cycle is too long (for example, one year), transactions and transfers may surge as the expiration time approaches, creating system overload. (A longer cycle can also benefit those looking to exploit others' seigniorage.) On the other hand, if the cycle is too short (for example, one minute), this can lead to a heavy system load and complicated calculations that confuse users. According to the rule of threes, humans can survive about 3 minutes without air, 3 days without water, and 3 weeks without food. Therefore, to allow people to buy essential items like drinking water during emergencies, a cycle within three days seems appropriate. A day is a common time unit for contracts (Chapter 8.4), making it less confusing for people and, most importantly, very intuitive.


What would a world where UIDC is successfully established look like? The value of UIDC as money is proportional to users' productivity (Chapter 8.2). Moreover, with repeated innovations and technological development (Chapter 8.3), humanity's productivity has been improving at an astonishing rate and continues to do so. As we have seen, the total balance of UIDC will converge to the number of users × 1,000 IDC. Assuming factors such as the velocity of money remain stable, as the average production per person grows, the purchasing power of UIDC will rise, leading to deflation (drop in prices) in the long run. Since the total amount of money is fixed, the more goods are produced, the more goods can be purchased with the same amount of money.


One advantage of UIDC is that once its purchasing power exceeds a certain level, there is no need to provide users with additional equipment (like internet-connected smartphones) for using UIDC. Let’s say the value of 1 IDC reaches 1 US dollar. Even a person without income could spend about $30 a month with this. This is manageable with an inexpensive smartphone and minimal internet access. They might spend nearly all of the UIDC issued from their wallet on smartphone maintenance, but the productivity of someone who can access the internet and make transfers would far surpass that of someone who cannot, becoming significant capital (Chapter 8.1). It would provide a tool to access the vast amount of information and useful models humanity has accumulated, as well as a convenient means of transaction.


However, this includes the hope that internet access will be possible from anywhere on Earth. Fortunately, the outlook for this hope is quite optimistic. First, UIDC is expected to increase the willingness to pay for internet access in all human habitats (excluding closed countries), creating incentives for producers to enter the market and offer services (Chapter 8.5) and to develop related technologies (Chapter 8.3). Furthermore, humanity's technology has reached a level where it can provide internet access across the entire surface of the Earth. Right now, top IT entrepreneurs are eagerly entering the low-orbit satellite internet market, and satellite antennas have become small enough to fit in a bag. Similarly, solar panels and batteries, also portable, can provide the power needed to run the antenna and smartphone. Soon, it will be possible to access the internet from any place with a view of the sky.


If the value of 1 IDC rises to around $5, extreme poverty will disappear. In 2025, the World Bank, a UN agency, updated the international poverty line (the threshold for extreme poverty) to $3 per day (based on 2021 PPP). According to estimates from 2022, about 838 million people worldwide lived in extreme poverty. If $5 worth of UIDC is issued daily from citizen wallets, even if about $1 is used for smartphone maintenance, over $3 would remain, thereby solving extreme poverty. This money could be used by poor people to buy essentials and increase their productivity, breaking the cycle of poverty (Chapter 9.2).


Of course, UIDC will not only bring positive results. In regions where the government fails to fulfill its role, people may need to establish self-defense groups to protect their property, like in the riverside village story in Chapter 9.3. Even with my limited imagination, I can envision scenarios where violent groups exploit other people's seigniorage, or where orphanages focus more on siphoning off seigniorage than on caring for children—even going so far as to conceal the tragic deaths of those in poor facilities. The choice to implement UIDC will create a world filled with both positive and negative outcomes (Chapter 2.2). Nevertheless, if a genius nurtured under the UIDC system generates significant positive externalities on an international scale (Chapter 9.4), then it would not be a losing investment for citizens of major advanced countries who see a larger amount of money disappear because they have more of it.


So, what does UIDC have to do with artificial intelligence? Producers use factors of production like land, labor, and capital to create goods (Chapter 8.1). In doing so, producers develop technology and innovate to reduce or substitute the input factors (Chapter 8.3). Yes, labor is one of those substitute factors. For example, before recording technology existed, a singer had to perform every time a transaction occurred, but now they can record a song once and sell it many times without singing again. People can carry 1,000 songs in their pockets. Humanity has continually sought to produce more goods with less effort and labor. Many jobs have disappeared from history in this process, while new ones have emerged. The fact that comput-er was originally the name of a job has become a topic of casual conversation.


The issue is that people are finding it increasingly difficult to cope with the rapid pace of innovation. This applies to both the speed at which innovations spread and the frequency with which they occur. Highly advanced transportation and communication systems are integrating the world into a single market, which in turn accelerates the spread of innovations (Chapter 8.3). Innovations that emerge anywhere in the world today can reach other continents within months through trade (Chapter 9.4) and communication networks. Moreover, since most countries are now part of international treaties protecting patent rights (Chapter 9.4), people no longer need to redundantly develop inventions that have already been created in other countries (Chapter 9.1).


Above all, science is a foundational field of study with low excludability (Chapter 9.1). Through language (Chapter 2.4), transportation, and communication, people around the world share the latest scientific knowledge, collaborate on research, publish findings simultaneously, and then share those results once more. Once shared, these useful scientific theories form the basis for new advancements in engineering and technology (Chapter 2.5), and advanced technologies enable more sophisticated scientific experiments and validations. Thus, technology develops on the foundation of accumulated knowledge and skills, accelerating at an exponential rate, much like compound interest. It took about 2,000 years for the Bronze Age (around 3300 BCE) to transition into the Iron Age (around 1200 BCE), but less than a century passed from the Wright brothers’ development of powered flight (1903) to Apollo 11 sending humans to the moon (1969).


The integrated market and shared scientific knowledge lead to increasingly frequent instances of creative destruction (Chapter 8.3) and, as a result, to technological unemployment (Chapter 9.2). In such cases, an oppressive government might attempt to prevent technological unemployment by restricting private-sector innovation and productivity improvements. For instance, banning the playback of recorded music would reduce singers' productivity, thereby creating more work opportunities. The stated legislative purpose (Chapter 7.1) might be to prevent noise from excessive playback or to suggest that unrestricted access to music diminishes its emotional impact. As the 19th-century economist Frédéric Bastiat pointed out, cutting off everyone’s right hand would effectively create more jobs.


If we want to continue enjoying the benefits of innovation while preventing people from falling into cycles of economic hardship, one approach could be to utilize welfare systems like those in the Meta-Advanced Country. A well-designed welfare system can support citizens facing technological unemployment in their search for new job opportunities (Chapter 9.2). It would be interesting to observe which types of countries—those that restrict innovation, those that implement welfare systems, or those that take no action against technological unemployment—will remain as future advanced countries (the countries people want to live in).


Artificial intelligence is merely an extension of this massive trend. However, what worries people is that this time, the range of jobs that could be replaced might expand to include everything humans can do. This means that in every field, the cost of human labor will become more expensive than the cost of purchasing or renting a robot that can replace it. Let's call this technological humanity unemployment. Producers looking to reduce production costs (Chapter 8.3) will use robots to create goods, while consumers wanting to buy goods at lower prices (Chapter 8.2) will purchase items produced by robots. Consequently, those who cannot own or rent robots will find it difficult to participate in transactions within the market. First, they will be excluded from the market as producers, and there will be no new market for them to enter as producers. Since they can't earn money to consume, they will soon be excluded from the market as consumers as well. Thus, a tragic irony arises; humanity has the ability to produce goods without the burdens of labor, yet this potential remains untapped.


When the value of 1 IDC is around $50, there will likely be people who are satisfied not to work while consuming $50 worth of goods daily. These are individuals who perceive the pain of additional labor to be greater than the utility gained from extra income. At $500, a few workaholics might still want to work. As Joseph Schumpeter mentioned in Chapter 8.3, "unemployment would lose practically all its terror if the private life of the unemployed were not seriously affected by their unemployment."


It is impossible to predict precisely when and how any innovation will occur (Chapter 9.2), including technological humanity unemployment. Another advantage of UIDC is that it removes the need to predict the timing and impact of various innovations. As we have seen, the purchasing power of UIDC is automatically linked to humanity's total production of goods. If the value of 1 IDC rises to $500 while the number of workers declines, leaving only a very few, it would indicate that goods worth at least the population × $500 are being produced by those remaining workers and consumed daily. By definition, utility comes not from labor but from the consumption of final goods (Chapter 8.1).


The realization of UIDC requires immense cooperation among world citizens. If successful, it will constantly provide us with economic opportunities and serve as a useful tool to combat extreme poverty and the unpredictable threat of technological unemployment.
